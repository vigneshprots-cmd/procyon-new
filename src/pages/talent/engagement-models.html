Our view

The model decides who carries the risk.

Fixed price moves scope risk to the partner, which is why fixed bids carry contingency for every unknown. Time and materials leaves the risk with the client, in exchange for the freedom to change course. Neither is better in general; each is wrong for some work.

A mismatched model rarely shows on day one. It shows in month four, as an argument over a change request or a burn rate nobody planned for. These are the four mismatches seen most often.

  • Fixed price on an unclear scope

    The partner prices the unknowns in, then defends the boundary of every requirement. The client pays for contingency and still gets change requests.First move: a short discovery phase first, then fix the price

  • Time and materials with no owner

    Flexible capacity without a client product owner setting priorities each week turns into effort without direction.First move: a named owner for the backlog before the first invoice

  • Outcome-based on a number nobody measures

    If the baseline was never measured, nobody can prove the KPI moved, and the incentive becomes a dispute.First move: measure the baseline before signing

  • Managed capacity for a one-off project

    A standing team for work with an end date means paying for capacity after the work is done.First move: switch models at go-live, not before

The comparison

Where each model fits, and where it backfires.

Any model works with staff augmentation, delivery pods or a project team. The question is what the work looks like, not who does it.

One price for defined deliverables

Fixed scope

Fits when the outcome is clear, such as a cloud rollout or an accelerator, and finance needs one number before signing. Governed by a scope document, phase gates and change control.

Backfires whenScope still moving. Every new idea becomes a change request, and both sides spend the program negotiating the boundary instead of delivering.

Billed on time used at agreed role rates

Time and materials

Fits when the roadmap will change, or AI use cases are still being explored, and locking scope now would mean paying for change requests. Governed by timesheets, a weekly backlog review and monthly reporting.

Backfires whenA fixed budget with no owner. Without a client owner steering priorities week to week, spend follows effort rather than value, and the budget runs out before the scope does.

Payment per signed-off deliverable

Milestone-based

Fits when the budget must be fixed, but some detail will only become clear once the program is running. Six phase gates, from discovery to optimization, each released by a signed deliverable.

Backfires whenMilestones nobody can sign. If acceptance criteria are vague, sign-off stalls, payments stall with it, and the gate turns into a negotiation.

Part of the fee tied to a KPI

Outcome-based

Fits when one business metric matters most, such as service response time or win rate, and leadership wants the partner to share the risk. Governed by a baseline, a target and an agreed measurement method.

Backfires whenA metric the partner cannot move. When the KPI depends mostly on things outside the work, such as pricing, staffing or the market, the fee tracks luck rather than delivery.

A pod or squad on recurring terms

Managed capacity

Fits when the work never really ends: a roadmap that keeps growing, or a live platform that has to keep improving every month. Governed by quarterly business reviews and a monthly capacity check.

Backfires whenA project with an end date. A standing team for finite work keeps billing after the work is done. Use fixed scope or milestones, then switch at go-live.

Two or more models

Blended

Most programs mix two: a fixed-scope build with time and materials for the discovery around it, or milestones for delivery and managed capacity after go-live. The written recommendation says which mix fits.

Backfires whenNobody owns the seams. Two models on one program need one change-control process and one reporting line, or work falls between them.

Before signing

Four questions that decide whether the model works.

Most commercial disputes trace back to one of these being left vague. The written recommendation answers each one for the program in question.

  • Who owns the backlog?A named client owner who sets priorities. Without one, time and materials drifts and fixed scope argues.
  • What counts as accepted?Acceptance criteria written per deliverable, so sign-off and payment do not depend on a meeting going well.
  • How is change priced?Change control agreed up front: who can raise a change, how it is estimated and who approves it.
  • When does the model switch?The trigger for moving from build to run terms, usually go-live, written into the first contract.

Which engagement models does Procyon offer?

Five: fixed scope, time and materials, milestone-based, outcome-based with part of the fee tied to business KPIs, and managed capacity with shared accountability.

What is the difference between fixed price and time and materials?

Fixed price sets one cost for defined deliverables and a timeline, with change requests for anything new. Time and materials bills for time used at agreed rates, and suits work where the scope is still evolving.

How does an outcome-based contract work?

The baseline for an agreed KPI, such as service response time or win rate, is measured first. Part of the fee is then tied to moving that KPI.

Can onshore, nearshore and offshore people be blended?

Yes. Teams can blend onshore people in the US, nearshore people in Canada and offshore people in India, at predictable rates.

Can the model change during a program?

Yes. A program can start on fixed scope or milestones and move to managed capacity after go-live, as the work shifts from building to running.
