Dreamforce 2026 Key Takeaways: Seven Agentforce Agents, AIForce & What Enterprises Do Next

Seven named agents, a runtime that holds a goal for weeks, and Salesforce data pushed well beyond its own interface. Here is what Dreamforce 2026 actually announced, what it changes for your architecture, and the 90-day plan we would run in your org.

Enterprises have largely settled the question of whether they will use AI. Across three days at Moscone Center the questions that moved were narrower, and far more useful to anyone holding a budget: where should AI operate, how does it reach trustworthy enterprise context, what is it allowed to do, and how does anyone prove it worked.

Procyon's Salesforce and enterprise AI practice was there for all three days. Where a claim below comes from Salesforce we say so. Where it is our reading, we say that too.

Dreamforce 2026 in one minute

Three words summarise what we took away from it.

Placement. Salesforce wants its data and its business capabilities available outside the Salesforce interface, in Slack, in AI assistants, in whatever application your people already have open.

Plumbing. An agent is only as useful as the systems it can reach. Almost every valuable workflow crosses an application boundary, so integration stopped being a supporting act.

Proof. Salesforce put named customer outcomes at the centre of its Agentforce material this year: deflection rates, autonomous resolution, cases handled at volume. That matches what enterprise buyers ask us for, which is a number measured against a baseline rather than a capability shown on stage.

Put those together and the shift is clear enough: the model is becoming the interchangeable part, while trusted data, integration architecture, identity and governance become the hard problems.

What Salesforce actually announced

The headline was AIforce, a live interface layer that makes Salesforce data, workflows, business logic, semantics, permissions, security and governance available inside other AI interfaces. It launched with three surfaces: ClaudeForce, which puts Salesforce inside Claude; Slackforce, which brings Salesforce context into Slack; and Agentforce Coworker, an AI teammate inside Lightning. The banner across Moscone listed five model partners: Anthropic, AWS, Gemini, NVIDIA and OpenAI.

Underneath sits Headless 360, which exposes governed Salesforce capabilities to authorised agents and applications, including over the Model Context Protocol. If you own the architecture, this is the announcement that changes your diagram.

Salesforce also introduced Koa, its first CRM reasoning model, post-trained from NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Super on Salesforce data and aimed at multi-step CRM work such as updating an opportunity, routing a case or scheduling a follow-up. On Salesforce's own CRM benchmark it matches or exceeds leading models with three times fewer errors, and both post-training and inference stay inside the Salesforce trust boundary. Pilot customers include Formula 1, Xero, UChicago Medicine and Baxter Credit Union, with general availability expected in winter 2026 in US regions.

Day two was about deployment rather than launch. Slackforce Surfaces reached general availability, alongside Slackbot in Lightning Experience, Big Mode and Slack Code. The Data 360 keynote concentrated on real-time context. AgentExchange opened as a marketplace for prebuilt agents and actions.

Day three belonged to the people who run the platform. The Admin keynote announced Builder Central, a no-code AI workspace that turns a plain-language description into a working app or agent and deploys it to a sandbox in one click, with automated testing and governance rules applied. The beta opened the week of 22 September 2026.

One honest note, because it matters when you read other recaps: Salesforce published no press releases dated 17 September. The third day was enablement and community, not product news. Any recap listing Day 3 announcements is filling a gap that was not there.

The seven named agents, and the runtime underneath them

Salesforce has been a platform for two decades, through Force.com, the API layer and AppExchange. What AIforce and Headless 360 change is where those capabilities get consumed: increasingly from front ends your team does not own or control. That is an identity and governance problem before it is an AI problem, and somebody has to answer, in writing, what an agent can see, what it can change, which systems it reaches through the same session, how every action is logged, and what happens when it is wrong.

Salesforce announced seven job-ready agents on 11 September, four days before the conference opened, and they framed a good deal of what followed. They are also the part of the week most likely to reach a budget conversation, because each one maps to a job somebody in your organisation already owns.

  • Casey resolves customer service issues across voice, SMS, WhatsApp and web chat. Generally available.
  • Paige resolves employee requests across Slack, portals and the tools people already use. Generally available.
  • Carter helps shoppers discover and compare products, answers their questions, and converts with in-chat checkout. Generally available.
  • Piper works across websites and inboxes to engage, qualify and convert inbound leads. Generally available.
  • Fin resolves complex customer experience workflows across every channel. Generally available.
  • Marshall orchestrates end-to-end back-office processes, automating manual work with deterministic execution. Generally available.
  • Hunter works a sales pipeline from research to outreach, collaborating with sellers over weeks and months. In pilot, with general availability expected in November 2026.

Salesforce published customer results alongside them: Anthropic resolving 79 percent of conversations autonomously with Fin, Hibbett covering 90 percent of core shopper journeys with Carter after a six-week launch, Autism Queensland resolving 70 percent of administrative requests with Paige, and Perk building 60 percent of its sales pipeline with Hunter.

Hunter is the one to read twice
Not because outbound sales is the biggest prize. Because Hunter is the one of the seven that cannot live inside a single conversation, so Salesforce had to build something underneath it: a long-horizon runtime that lets an agent, in Salesforce's own words, pursue goals across days and weeks instead of completing only a task or interaction.

Three pieces make that work. Memory preserves context and progress across sessions, so work does not stop when the interaction ends. Durable execution keeps a plan running over time and lets the agent resume or course-correct as circumstances change. Dynamic steering adapts the agent's behaviour to an individual user's feedback. Alongside it, Multi-Agent Orchestration routes work across agents so they operate as one coordinated team.

That is the announcement with the longest shadow, and it is worth being clear about why. A chat agent that gets something wrong produces a bad conversation. An agent that holds a goal for six weeks, resumes after interruptions, coordinates with other agents and adjusts its own plan is a participant in your process. It accumulates state nobody is looking at, acts across systems when no human is watching the thread, and its mistakes compound quietly instead of announcing themselves.

Everything in the rest of this article gets more important, not less, once the agent runs for a month.

The platform is now consumed from outside Salesforce

This is the change with the longest tail. Salesforce has been a platform for two decades, through Force.com, the API layer and AppExchange. What AIforce and Headless 360 change is where those platform capabilities get consumed. A governed Salesforce action can now be invoked from Slack, from Claude or from a custom application, which means increasingly from front ends your team does not own or control.

That is an identity and governance problem before it is an AI problem. If an agent running in Slack or Claude can reach Salesforce, somebody has to answer, in writing:

  • What Salesforce data can this agent see, and under whose permissions?
  • What actions can it take, and which of those need a human to approve?
  • Which systems beyond Salesforce can it reach through that same session?
  • How is every action logged, and who reviews the log?
  • What happens when the agent is wrong, and who notices?

Teams that already run mature enterprise integration and identity practices will find this a natural extension. Teams where Salesforce permissions grew organically over a decade will find it exposes years of accumulated drift. That audit is worth starting now, before an agent does it for you in production.

Context is the moat, not the model

Koa will get the headlines. It is not the part that decides whether any of this works in your organization.

An agent becomes valuable when it understands the customer, the contract, the support history, the entitlement, the business rule and the approval chain around the task in front of it. That understanding does not come from the model. It comes from your data.

Which is why the line that stayed with us was printed on the expo floor rather than delivered from a stage: get your data right to get your AI right. An agent working over clean, connected, governed data automates real work. An agent working over duplicate accounts, half-populated fields, stale integrations and permissions nobody has reviewed since 2019 does not fail safely. It executes the wrong action faster, at scale, with an audit trail saying a human approved the design.

So the question before choosing a model is not which model. It is whether your enterprise context is trustworthy enough for something to act on, across Salesforce, ERP, billing, the customer data platform, the warehouse and whatever industry system actually holds the truth. That is ordinary data engineering and analytics work, and it is the gate.

Agentforce moved from demos to numbers, with one gap

The operational evidence is now substantial. Salesforce reports 3.2 billion Agentic Work Units completed in Q2, up 97 percent on Q1, across more than 30,000 customers in 124 countries.

The named results are more useful than the aggregate:

  • Live Nation at a 95 percent case deflection rate.
  • Xero resolving 62 percent of support cases autonomously across more than 800,000 cases a year.
  • Siemens engaging 100 percent of inbound leads across 132 countries for 18,000 sellers.

Here is the gap, and it is an industry gap rather than a Salesforce one. Containment and deflection are activity measures. They tell you what the agent handled. They do not tell you the fully loaded cost of running it, or what it contributed to margin. Very few published case studies close that loop.

Which means you have to close it yourself, and you can only do that from a baseline recorded before anything is deployed: current volume, current cost to serve, current handling time, current resolution rate, current human effort, and the expected cost to run the agent including inference, integration, monitoring and escalation. Measure the same workflow afterwards on the same definitions. Attach the return to the workflow, never to the platform.

Governance has to arrive before agent sprawl

Builder Central lowers the cost of building an agent sharply, and that is precisely why it deserves a governance conversation this quarter rather than next year.

When building an agent required a developer, scarcity did your governance for you. When an administrator can describe one in a sentence and deploy it in a click, that constraint disappears, and organisations can go from three agents to thirty faster than their operating model adapts. The failure mode is the same one Lightning components, flows and integrations all produced: a large estate nobody owns.

Every production agent needs an answer to five questions before it goes live. Who owns it. Who approves what it can access. What it may do without a human. How its performance is monitored. When it gets retired. None of that slows adoption down. It is what lets adoption keep going after the first incident.

The goal is not to govern AI cautiously. It is to make governance scale as fast as building does.

Your 90-day plan after Dreamforce 2026

You do not need to wait for every announced product to reach general availability. Almost all of the preparatory work is available to start now, and most of it is work you would want done regardless.

Days 1 to 30: assess

Identify five to ten workflows that are high volume, measurably painful and owned by somebody who cares. For each one, assess the Salesforce architecture and technical debt behind it, the quality and completeness of the data it depends on, its integration dependencies, and its identity and permission model. Write down explicitly what an agent must never do in that workflow without human approval.

The aim is not a catalogue of every possible AI use case. It is to find the small number where AI would produce a number your CFO recognises.

Days 31 to 60: prove

Pick one to three. Before a line of configuration, assign a business owner, record the baseline, define the success metric, design the human escalation path, and collect the real edge cases from the people who currently do the work. Then build, and test against those edge cases rather than the happy path.

A proof of concept that only proves the technology works has not proved anything commercially interesting. It has to prove a business outcome.

Days 61 to 90: operationalise

Move what worked into controlled production and measure against the original baseline. Review the exceptions, because that is where the next design lives. Then extract the reusable pattern: integration approach, permission model, monitoring, escalation design, documentation.

The objective of the first ninety days is not the maximum number of agents. It is one repeatable enterprise pattern for deploying them safely and profitably. The second workflow should take a third of the time the first one did. If it does not, the pattern is not finished.
