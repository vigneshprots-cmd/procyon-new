Enterprise AI Beyond the Pilot: How to Prioritize Use Cases That Pay Off

Most enterprises don't have an AI idea problem. They have a prioritization problem. Here's how to pick the use cases that actually pay off.

Walk into most US enterprises today and you will not find a shortage of AI ideas. You will find a whiteboard with forty of them, a handful of pilots that impressed a demo audience, and a quiet question no one wants to ask out loud: which of these is actually worth taking to production? The bottleneck is rarely imagination. It is prioritization. This is a practical framework for choosing AI use cases the way the strongest enterprises do - by value and feasibility, not by novelty.

BY THE NUMBERS

Gartner projects that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, largely for unclear business value - proof that the constraint is rarely ideas, and almost always prioritization. Source: Gartner

Why AI Pilots Die in the Middle

The graveyard of enterprise AI is full of technically successful pilots. They died not because the model was wrong, but because the use case was chosen for how impressive it looked, not for the value it created or the odds it could ship. Three patterns repeat: no clear business owner, data that was never ready, and no one owning the path from demo to production. Prioritization done well removes all three before a single model is trained.

The Two Axes That Matter: Value and Feasibility

Axis

Ask

Business value

If this worked, would a leader point to a real outcome - revenue, cycle time, service quality, risk reduction? Can it be measured?

Feasibility

Is the data available and reasonably clean? How complex is the build? How much change does it demand of the people who must adopt it?

The Prioritization Grid

1
High value, high feasibility

Do first. These build momentum, credibility, and the internal case for the harder work. Your best first project lives here.

2
High value, low feasibility

Plan and invest. Worth doing, but fix the data and change readiness first. Roadmap items, not quick wins.

3
Low value, high feasibility

Selective quick wins. Fine for learning, but do not let them crowd out the high-value work.

4
Low value, low feasibility

Decline. Where impressive demos go to consume budget. Say no early.

START WHERE VALUE AND FEASIBILITY MEET

The single most reliable predictor of an enterprise AI program that scales is that its first shipped project was chosen from the high-value, high-feasibility quadrant. Early credibility funds everything that follows.

Feasibility Is Mostly a Data Question

When feasibility scores come back low, the reason is usually data - incomplete, scattered, or untrustworthy. This is why data readiness is not a prerequisite you can skip; it is the foundation the entire program stands on. If a high-value use case is blocked, the fix is often a targeted data effort, not a better model. Our data strategy and analytics services exist precisely to unblock these cases.

THE NOVELTY TRAP

The use case that gets the most excited nods is frequently the one with the least measurable value. Guard against choosing projects by applause. Make every candidate declare its owner and its metric before it competes for funding.

From Grid to Roadmap

  1. Inventory the candidates across the business, not just from IT.

  2. Score each on value and feasibility with the people who own the outcome in the room.

  3. Ship one high-value, high-feasibility project end to end, with a named owner and a metric.

  4. Reinvest the credibility into the high-value, low-feasibility bets by funding the data work they need.

  5. Govern from day one so production AI is monitored and owned, not just launched.

Questions this article answers.

How should enterprises prioritize AI use cases?

Score each candidate on two axes: business value and feasibility. Feasibility includes data readiness, technical complexity, and organizational change required. Start with high-value, high-feasibility use cases to build momentum before tackling harder bets.

Why do most enterprise AI pilots fail to scale?

Most pilots stall not because the model underperforms but because the use case was chosen for novelty rather than value, the data was not ready, or no one owned the path to production. Prioritizing for value and feasibility up front prevents most of these failures.

What makes an AI use case a good first project?

A strong first project has a clear business owner, a measurable outcome, data that already exists and is reasonably clean, and a manageable change footprint. It should be valuable enough to matter but contained enough to ship in a quarter.
